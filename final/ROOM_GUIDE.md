# What each room tests, and what the participant actually does

A companion to `README.md`. That file explains how the system is built; this one
explains what a participant is being asked to do once they are standing in it,
room by room, and what each room is actually measuring.

---

## The one thing you do everywhere

**Look around, find anything that could cause a fall, point at it and pull the
trigger.** In VR that is the right-hand trigger; on the desktop it is a left
click, with a crosshair showing what you are pointing at.

That is the whole task. There is no time limit, no score shown while you play,
and nothing to type. Everything else in this guide is a description of what is
in each room for you to find.

When you flag something correctly it glows green, its name appears floating
beside it, and the reason is read aloud — "Television lead across the floor. Run
leads around the skirting board, never across the floor." The count at the top of
the instruction panel goes up.

**The yellow column of light** standing in a doorway is the focus beacon. From
your second session onwards it stands outside whichever room you scored lowest on
recently, so you start with the room that needs the practice. It disappears once
you walk in. It is a suggestion, never a gate — you can do the rooms in any
order.

**Some objects are meant to be safe.** At least one in each room is a decoy, and
the street has two: the good armchair, the hall table, the towel rail, the
wardrobe, the kettle, the postbox and the litter bin.
Pointing at one costs you nothing — it is logged for the researcher, and
acknowledged with a neutral tone rather than a buzz, because being a bit
over-cautious is a habit worth keeping. Noticing *why* they are safe is the
actual lesson.

## The second thing you do: cross the room

Finding a hazard and getting past one are different skills. Someone can point at
a coffee table, name it correctly, and then walk their shin into it.

So once the session has started, **one amber ring appears on the floor** of the
room you are standing in. Stand on it: it turns green for a moment, disappears,
and the next one appears somewhere else in the room. You only ever see one ring
at a time, and only in the room you are in -- nothing is drawn in the rooms you
have not got to yet, and a room you walked out of half-way through goes dark
until you come back to it.

The stretch between two rings is a *leg*, and a leg is clean only if you neither
touched a hazard on the way nor chose a route straight through one.

The rings do not appear until you press **START** on the welcome panel. Before
that the house is empty: during the walk-in there is nothing on the floor to
read.

**Some legs are deliberately not walkable in a straight line.** The rug edge runs
across the hall; the cupboard door stands open across the kitchen; the lead runs
across the living-room floor. The direct line goes through them, and the task is
to notice that and go round. Every leg in the house was checked to have a clear
path before it was placed, so there is always a way.

Nothing is enforced. Ignore the rings entirely and the session carries on — you
simply score nothing for the route in that room.

**Some hazards warn you as you approach.** Come within about half a metre of the
lead, the wet floor or the lifted paving without having spotted it and a voice
says so — "Mind your feet. There is a lead across the floor here." **The warning
costs you nothing.** It is the part of the system that is trying to help, and
being warned early is the whole point of it.

**Only actually standing on the thing is a near miss**, and that is what costs a
few points and spoils the walking leg — because being told is not the same as
noticing. The two volumes are deliberately different sizes: you are warned off a
wide area and penalised only on the object itself.

## Living room — getting out of a chair, and what is on the floor

The room where people spend the most time, and where two of the most common
domestic falls happen: catching a foot on something low and dark, and failing to
stand up from a seat.

**Four things to find.**

The **sofa** is too low, too deep and too soft, and it has no arms. A seat like
that has to be stood up from with the legs alone, and "I fell getting up" is the
single most common fall in this age group. Stand next to it and look at how low
the seat is.

The **glass coffee table** is 0.38 m high and sits right on the route between the
door and the chairs. Low and see-through is the worst possible combination: the
eye does not register it and the shin finds it.

The **television lead** runs across the open floor instead of round the skirting.
This is the one that will warn you if you walk into it first.

The **footstool** has been left in the walkway just inside the door, where it was
last used rather than where it lives.

**The decoy is the armchair** — firm, higher seat, proper arms to push up on. It
is there on purpose, right next to the sofa, as the good example. The comparison
between the two seats is the lesson of this room.

**The route:** door → the plant → the side table → beside the sofa → the window.

Two of the four legs need a detour: round the footstool and the west end of the television lead on the way down the room, and then round the glass coffee table, which sits square in the middle. The last ring is a short step with nothing in the way, so the room ends on something you can get right.

## Hallway — the route you walk most, in the dark

The spine of the house, and the stretch you cross more often than any other,
often at night without the light on.

**Two things to find.** The **rug** has a curled, lifted edge that catches a toe
or the front of a walking frame. The **extension cord** has been run straight
across the walkway rather than along the skirting.

**The decoy is the hall table** — solid, against the wall, out of the way.

Fewer hazards here than elsewhere on purpose: the hallway is where you start and
where you pass through constantly, so it is deliberately not crowded.

**The route:** west end → past the hall table → the bare floor before the rug → past the rug → the far end.

No ring stands on the rug itself: the third one stops short of it, and the fourth is beyond it, so getting from one to the other is the whole point of the leg.

Two legs need a detour: the curled rug edge runs the full width of the hall, and the extension cord runs along it. Both have a way past; the cord's is on the south side.

## Kitchen — reaching, and what is underfoot

The kitchen tests the two ways a kitchen puts you off balance: reaching for
things, and standing on a wet floor.

**Four things to find.**

Everyday items are stored on a **high shelf**, above shoulder height. Reaching up
there takes your balance backwards over your heels, which is exactly where you
cannot recover it.

The **step stool** brought in to reach that shelf has nothing to hold on to. A
stool with no handrail is worse than the reach it saves.

There is a **spill on the floor**. Hard kitchen tile with water on it is as
slippery as the bathroom. This one warns you if you walk onto it.

A **cupboard door has been left standing open** across the walkway, at shin
height — below where you are looking when you are carrying something.

**The decoy is the kettle.** An ordinary kettle on the worktop, at a sensible
height, near an outlet. Safe.

**The route:** doorway → the step stool → past the open cupboard door → the worktop → the cooker.

Two legs need a detour: the open cupboard door sticks out across the path, and the spill covers the floor in front of the west counter. You cannot reach the sink without stepping in the spill — that is the point of it.

## Bathroom — the most dangerous room in the house

**Three things to find.**

The **tile floor is wet** — the single most slippery surface in a home.

There is **no grab bar at the end of the bath you actually step into**. Look at
where your hand would go as you lift a leg over the side: there is nothing there.

The **bath mat has no grip underneath**. A mat that slides on wet tile is worse
than no mat at all.

**The decoy is the towel rail** — a towel rail is for towels, and people grab
them as if they were grab bars, which is why it is here to be looked at and
dismissed.

**There is a real grab bar, and it is in the wrong place.** One is fitted along
the far side of the bath. You can see it and you can hold it — but it is not
where you step in, which is the hazard above. Nothing in the bathroom is scored
on rail use: marking you down for not holding a bar the scenario says is badly
placed would contradict the hazard you are being asked to spot. Rail compliance
is measured on the stairs, where the rail is where it should be, and 60 % of the
crossing spent holding is the pass mark there.

**The route:** doorway → beside the bath → the basin.

One leg needs a detour: the wet floor covers most of the middle of the room, and the bath mat sits on the route out of the bath.

## Staircase — the one place the simulator makes you do it properly

Stairs are the most serious fall risk in a house, so this is the only part of the
house where the software actually enforces the safe behaviour rather than just
scoring it.

**Three things to find.** The **handrail is missing on one side** — only the
brackets are left. The **stairwell light is too dim** to judge a step by. And one
**tread is taller than the others**, which is the classic stair trip.

**You cannot teleport past the stairs.** Free-aim teleport is switched off inside
the stairwell. The only legal destinations are the marked discs on the treads,
and a disc only lights up while you are **actually holding the rail**, and only if
it is the next one up or down from where you stand. So the flight is taken one
landing at a time, with a hand on the rail, in both directions. There is no aim
that skips it.

If you cannot get the next disc to light: you have let go of the rail. Hold grip
(`Space`) and it will come up green.

## Bedroom — the trip to the door at night

**Three things to find.**

The **bedside chair has no armrests**, so there is nothing to push up on — the
same lesson as the living-room sofa, in the room where you get up half asleep.

The **light is too dim**. You need to be able to see the floor before your feet
touch it. (The room is genuinely dark, the curtains are drawn and the ceiling
pendant is modelled unlit — that is the hazard, not a bug.)

There is **clutter on the floor between the bed and the door**: a box, shoes,
books. That is the route you take at night, in the dark, and it needs to be
completely clear.

**The decoy is the wardrobe** — large and heavy, but against the wall and out of
the walking route.

**The route:** doorway → past the clutter → beside the bed → the wardrobe.

The first leg needs a detour: the clutter sits directly between the door and the
middle of the room, which is exactly the line you walk at night. The room has
one obstacle on its floor, so it gets one awkward leg; padding that out with
rings that curve round nothing would not teach anything.

## Street — the part most training leaves out

People do not only fall indoors, and the outdoor falls are rarely about the
surface. They are about how a crossing is approached.

**Eight things to find.** The **step down at the front door**, easy to forget on
the way out. A **lifted paving slab**. **Wet leaves**, which are as slippery as
ice. A **high kerb with no dropped edge**. A **wheelie bin left blocking the
pavement**, which pushes you into the road to get past it. An **open drain** in
the front path, a real hole with its cast iron rim round it — the grating that
belongs over it is leaning against the garden wall where somebody left it. A
**shop A-board** stood across the pavement. And the **low limb of the
street tree**, hanging over the pavement at head height — the one hazard out
here you have to look up to find.

**Two decoys: the postbox and the litter bin.** Both are where they should be,
clear of the walking line.

### Two practices out here, in order

**Practice one: walking the pavement.** The same walking route as every room
indoors. Green rings, one at a time, from the gate along the pavement and back
to the crossing. All four legs have something in the way — the wet leaves just
outside the gate, the wheelie bin, the lifted slab, then the bin again from the
other side — so every one of them has to be gone round rather than through. The
last ring puts you at the crossing, next to the button.

**Practice two: the crossing.** It starts when you press the button, and it is
described below. It is a **separate practice with its own score** — "Road
crossing" on the performance board — because reading a set of lights and
watching the ground three steps ahead are two different skills, and the board
has to be able to say which of the two needs the work.

### The crossing has lights, and using them is the exercise

The road is 52 m of two-lane street with **four cars** on it, two each way. The
crossing is signalled, and it works the way a real one does:

1. **Press the button** on the pole at the edge of the crossing. That also
   starts the practice. The light does
   not change straight away — the traffic gets its minimum green first. That
   delay is deliberate; a press that stopped the traffic instantly would teach
   the wrong thing.
2. **Wait.** Amber for the traffic, then a moment with everything stopped.
3. **The green man shows, and bleeps.** This is when to start. You have ten
   seconds of it, with the seconds counting down on the post.
4. **The green man flashes.** Finish if you are already crossing. Do not start.
5. Back to green for the traffic.

If you stand at the kerb without pressing, nothing happens — the lights rest on
green until somebody asks. A voice reminds you what to do every fourteen seconds
rather than cycling on its own, because a crossing that changes by itself
teaches you that the button does not matter.

**Two different things are measured, and both feed the Road crossing score** —
the run counts for 65 % of it and the kerb for 35 %.

The first is the *moment you step off the kerb*, out of 100:

| | |
|---|---|
| 35 | **waited for the green man** — stepped out on a steady green man, not a red one and not a flashing one |
| 25 | **looked both ways** — turned your head far enough each way, recently enough to count, **before** stepping off the kerb |
| 20 | stepped into the road **at the marked crossing**, not between parked cars |
| 20 | **left enough time** — no car was close, or they were already past |

Looking both ways still counts for a quarter of it even on a green man, because
a green man is not a guarantee.

The second is *the crossing itself* — the run from kerb to kerb, which is a
different skill and catches different mistakes:

| | |
|---|---|
| 50 | **got across in time** — reached the far pavement before the traffic got its green back. Finishing on the flashing man scores 40 of the 50; that is normal and correct |
| 25 | **set off promptly** — left the kerb within a few seconds of the green man appearing. Hesitating is what runs the clock out |
| 25 | **kept to the markings** — stayed between the two white edge lines the whole way over |

Rings appear across the crossing when the green man does: step on each in turn
and they take you straight across between the lines. The first one is where you
wait, and the last one is on the pavement at the other end.

**To come back, press the button on the pole on that side.** The lights do the
same thing, the rings appear again running the other way, and the crossing is
scored exactly as the first one was. You can go back and forth as often as you
like. Press the button and then
not move at all scores nothing — the practice is about going when it is your
turn.

Nothing stops you walking into the road anywhere you like, on any colour. The
cars will still see you and stop — none of them will ever touch you — but the
attempt is scored as what it was, and you are told so. A training tool that
makes the mistake impossible cannot tell you whether you would have made it.

## Night

There is a **Night mode** button on the long north wall of the hallway, on its
own, past the stairs. Press it and the same house goes dark: the daylight goes, the
lamps come on, and the street outside is lit by its lamp posts.

It is worth doing the whole walk again this way. Two of the hazards barely read
in daylight — the bedroom light that is too dim to see the floor by, and the
unlit stairwell — because in daylight they are not hazards. The lamps keep their
own brightness when night falls, so the feeble ones stay feeble, and those two
become the most obvious things in the house.

This is also the version that matches when falls actually happen: on the way to
the bathroom, in the middle of the night.

Press it again for daylight. It can be pressed at any point, including in the
middle of a session, and it does not affect scoring.

Beside it, in dark red, is **Reset scores**. It erases every score saved for the
current participant, so the board goes blank and the next session counts as a
first one. It takes two presses: the first changes the label to "Press again to
erase" and tells you what is about to happen, and it forgets about it after six
seconds if you walk away. There is no undo, and it does not stop or change the
session you are in the middle of.

## The balance exercise

Not a room. After the house and the street, press the purple **Balance exercise**
button on the east wall.

Stand still, feet where they are, looking ahead. A countdown from three gives you
time to settle and tells you exactly when measuring starts. A glowing ball
appears at arm's length; reach out and touch it, **only as far as is
comfortable**. Three trials, one ahead and one to each side. If you cannot reach
one, that is fine — just wait, and it is recorded as not completed rather than as
a failure.

What is measured is how much your head travels while you reach, relative to where
it was at "go" — a proxy for postural sway, loosely modelled on the clinical
Functional Reach Test. It is not clinical-grade, and the debrief says so.

## How it adds up

Each room gets its own score out of 100, and so do the street, the road crossing
and the balance task. They appear on the performance board on the wall, with last session's score
beside today's.

| Test | How it is scored |
|---|---|
| Each room | 50 % hazards found there + 20 % rail use there + 30 % route cleanliness, minus 10 per near miss (capped at −20) |
| Street | 60 % hazards found outdoors + 40 % the pavement route, same near-miss penalty |
| Road crossing | 65 % the crossing runs + 35 % how you stepped off the kerb, renormalised if you only did one of the two |
| Balance | mean steadiness of completed reaches × the fraction you completed |
| Overall | 50 % hazard recognition + 30 % handrail use + 20 % balance |

Any part a room cannot test is dropped and the rest renormalised, so a room with
no handrail is scored on hazards and route alone. The road crossing says "not
done this session" rather than nought if you never took one: going out for a
walk and coming home again is not a failed crossing. The stairs are the only place where
rail use counts — see the bathroom section for why its grab bar is not scored —
and the staircase has no route of its own, because its gated step discs already
are one, with stricter rules.

Route cleanliness is the share of a room's legs crossed without touching or
routing through a hazard — judged on the path you actually walked, so going
round something counts as going round it. Legs you never attempt count against it, so walking out
half way is not the same as walking it cleanly — and a room whose rings you
ignore entirely scores zero for the route, which is what stops skipping the task
being the easy way to a good score.

**From the second session onwards** the lowest-scoring test under 90, averaged
over the last three sessions, becomes today's focus: the board names it, the
instructions mention it, and the yellow beacon stands at its doorway. That is the
adaptive part — the house does not change, but where it points you does.
