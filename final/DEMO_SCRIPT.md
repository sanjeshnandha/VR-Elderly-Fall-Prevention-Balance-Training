# Demo video — full walkthrough narration

Roughly thirteen to fifteen minutes at a normal speaking pace. Every room, every
hazard, both street practices, the balance exercise and the scoring.

Short sentences on purpose: easier to read aloud, easier to follow. Lines in
*[brackets]* are what to show on screen, not words to say. There is a
ninety-second cut-down at the end.

---

## 1. Opening  (~40 s)

*[Title card, then a headset view of the hallway]*

Falls are the leading cause of injury for people over sixty-five. Most of them
do not happen somewhere unfamiliar. They happen at home, in rooms people have
lived in for twenty or thirty years, on a rug or a cord or a step they have
walked past every day.

This is a virtual reality training simulator for exactly that. It teaches older
adults to notice those hazards, and to get past them safely, before they meet
them for real.

It is built in Godot, it runs on any OpenXR headset, and a full session takes
about fifteen minutes.

## 2. How it works  (~70 s)

*[Press Start; the instruction panel appears; then a hazard being flagged]*

The participant puts on the headset and presses Start. Every instruction is
spoken aloud as well as shown on screen, because small text in a headset is not
something we should rely on.

There is no time limit and nothing here can hurt you.

Three things happen in every room.

First, you look for hazards. You point at something and pull the trigger. It
lights up, it is named out loud, and you are told in one sentence why it is a
risk.

Second, some of what you see is perfectly safe, and those are there on purpose.
Pointing at a safe object costs you nothing. It is logged for the researcher,
and you get a neutral tone rather than a buzz, because being a bit over-cautious
is a habit worth keeping.

Third, there is something to do, not just something to find. Green rings appear
on the floor, one at a time, and walking the route without touching anything is
scored in its own right.

Across the whole house there are twenty-seven real hazards and seven safe
objects. Let us walk through them.

## 3. The hallway  (~70 s)

*[Stand in the hallway; flag the rug, then the cord]*

The hallway is where the session starts, and it has the two classics.

The first is a rug with a curled edge. A lifted edge catches the toe of a shoe,
or the foot of a walking frame. The fix is to tape it down or take the rug up
altogether.

The second is an extension cord run straight across the walkway. Cords belong
along the skirting board, never across a path you use every day.

*[Point at the hall table]*

The safe object here is the hall table. It is solid, it is against the wall, and
it is out of the way. Nothing wrong with it.

*[The rings appear]*

Then the route. Five rings: along the hall, past the table, before the rug, past
the rug, and the far end. The rug sits across the middle of the hallway, so
there is no walking straight through it — you have to go round.

## 4. The living room  (~95 s)

*[Walk in; flag each hazard in turn]*

The living room has four, and they are the two ways people actually fall in
here: getting out of a seat, and catching a foot on something low and dark.

The sofa is too low and too soft to stand up from. A deep seat with no arms has
to be stood up from with the legs alone. Raise it on firm blocks, or sit in the
armchair instead.

The coffee table is low and made of glass. Low and see-through is the worst
combination there is — the eye does not register it and the shin finds it. It
needs to come out of the route between the door and the chairs.

The television lead runs across the floor. Same rule as the hallway: round the
skirting, never across the room. A lead you step over every day is one you will
eventually catch.

And the footstool has been left in the walkway. It belongs back beside the
chair. Anything low, left where you walk, is a trip waiting for the one time you
are not looking.

*[Point at the armchair]*

The safe object is the armchair — firm arms, a higher seat, something to push up
from. It is deliberately placed right beside the bad sofa, because the
difference between the two is the lesson.

*[The rings]*

Five rings here, and the route has to work around the coffee table, the lead and
the footstool.

## 5. The kitchen  (~90 s)

*[Walk in; flag each hazard]*

Four in the kitchen.

Everyday things have been stored too high. Reaching above your shoulders takes
your balance backwards over your heels. What you use daily should live between
hip and shoulder height.

Next to it is a wobbly step stool with nothing to hold. A stool with no handrail
is worse than the reach it saves. A steady step with a handle, and never a
chair.

There is a spill on the floor by the sink. Hard kitchen tile with water on it is
as slippery as the bathroom. Wipe it the moment it happens.

And a cupboard door has been left open across the walkway. A low door standing
open sits at shin height, below where you are looking. Close each one as you
finish with it.

*[Point at the kettle]*

The safe object is the kettle — an ordinary kettle on the worktop at a sensible
height, with nothing wrong with it at all.

*[The rings]*

Five rings, through a room where the spill and the open door are both on the
natural line between the door and the cooker.

## 6. The bathroom  (~80 s)

*[Walk in; flag each hazard]*

The bathroom is the smallest room and statistically one of the most dangerous.

The floor by the bath is wet. Wet tile is one of the most slippery surfaces in
the house. Dry it, or wait before stepping on it.

There is no grab bar at the end of the bath you actually step into. There is one
fitted at the far end — you can see it, and you can hold it — but it is in the
wrong place, which is the point. There is nothing solid to hold where you need
it.

And the bath mat has no grip underneath. A mat that slides on wet tile is worse
than no mat at all. It wants a rubber backing, or fixing down.

*[Point at the towel rail]*

The safe object is the towel rail. It is there because people grab towel rails
as if they were grab bars. It is for towels. It is worth looking at, and
dismissing.

*[The rings]*

Three rings: the doorway, beside the bath, the basin. One leg needs a detour,
because the wet floor covers most of the middle of the room.

## 7. The stairs  (~80 s)

*[Stand at the foot of the stairs; flag each hazard; then climb]*

The stairs have three hazards and one rule.

The handrail is missing on this side. Only the brackets are left. A broken rail
gets reported before the stairs are used, and until it is fixed you use the
other side.

The stairwell lighting is poor. You cannot judge a step you cannot see. That
needs a brighter bulb and a switch at both ends.

And one tread is taller than the rest. An uneven step is the one your foot
expects to be where the last one was. Look at each step, up and down.

*[Climb, holding the rail]*

The rule is the rail. You have to hold it — the next step only lights up while
you are holding on, so the habit is built into the exercise rather than
suggested.

And it measures how much of the climb you spent holding, not whether you touched
it once. Sixty per cent of the crossing is the pass mark.

## 8. The bedroom  (~70 s)

*[Walk in; flag each hazard]*

The bedroom is about one journey in particular: the one from the bed to the door
in the middle of the night.

The light is too dim. You need enough light to see the floor before your feet
touch it, and a lamp you can reach from the bed.

The chair has no armrests. With no arms you cannot push yourself up, so you end
up throwing your weight forward to stand.

And there is clutter on the path to the door. This is the route you take at
night, half asleep. It needs to be completely clear, from the bed all the way to
the door.

*[Point at the wardrobe]*

The safe object is the wardrobe. Large and heavy, but against the wall and out
of the way.

*[The rings]*

Four rings: the doorway, past the clutter, beside the bed, the wardrobe.

## 9. Outside: walking the pavement  (~110 s)

*[Through the front door, down the path]*

Outside is the part most training leaves out, and there are two separate
exercises out here. Each one is scored on its own.

The first is simply walking the pavement — and there are eight hazards on the
way.

It starts at the front door, where there is a step down. A single step on the
way out is easy to forget. Look down as you go through the door.

*[The open gully]*

In the front path there is an open drain. The grating is leaning against the
garden wall where somebody left it, and the hole is open. A gully with its cover
off is an ankle deep, in the one place you look at least — the path you have
walked a thousand times.

*[Through the gate, onto the pavement]*

On the pavement there are wet leaves. Wet leaves are as slippery as ice. Walk
round them, or brush them away before they pack down.

There is a paving slab lifted by a tree root. It catches the toe of a shoe or
the foot of a frame. That one gets reported to the council.

The wheelie bin has been left blocking the pavement. Anything that pushes you
off the pavement puts you in the road.

There is an advertising board stood across the pavement. It narrows the walking
line to less than a frame needs, and it sits below where you are looking.

*[Look up at the tree]*

There is a branch from the street tree hanging at head height. This is the one
hazard out here you have to look up to find. Catching your head makes you step
back — and stepping back is how people go over.

And at the far end there is a high kerb with no dropped edge. Stepping down a
full kerb takes more balance than it looks. The dropped kerb at the crossing is
the better route.

*[Point at the postbox and the litter bin]*

Two safe objects out here: the postbox and the litter bin. Both are against the
boundary, clear of the walking line.

*[The rings]*

Six rings along the pavement and back to the crossing, and every single leg has
something to go round.

## 10. Outside: the crossing  (~110 s)

*[At the crossing; press the button]*

The second street exercise is crossing the road, and it is a separate practice
with its own score.

It starts when you press the button on the pole. Nothing happens until you do —
the lights do not cycle on their own, because the lesson is that the button is
what starts it.

*[The signal sequence]*

Then it runs the way a real one does. The traffic gets amber, then everything
goes red, and then the green man appears, and it bleeps while it is safe to set
off.

*[Walk across]*

Rings appear across the crossing and take you from kerb to kerb between the
white lines.

*[The green man flashes]*

Then the green man flashes. If you are already crossing, finish. If you have not
started, wait for the next one.

*[On the far pavement, press the button on that pole]*

There is a button on the pole at this end too. Press it, and the whole thing
runs again the other way: the lights change, the rings appear going back, and
the crossing is scored the same. You can go back and forth as many times as you
want.

The traffic is real. Four cars, two each way. They approach, they slow down, and
they stop for anyone in the road — none of them will ever touch you.

And nothing stops you walking into the road anywhere you like, on any colour.
The attempt is simply scored as what it was, and you are told so. A training
tool that makes the mistake impossible cannot tell you whether you would have
made it.

## 10b. Night  (~50 s)

*[Back in the hallway, press the Night mode button; walk through a room or two]*

One button changes the time of day.

Most falls at home happen on the way to the bathroom in the middle of the night
— half asleep, no slippers, no glasses, and whatever light there is. So the same
house runs after dark.

*[Walk to the bedroom, then the stairs]*

The lamps keep their own brightness when the sun goes off. That matters, because
two of the hazards in this house are lights: the bedroom light that is too dim
to see the floor by, and the unlit stairwell. In daylight they barely register,
because in daylight they are not hazards. After dark they are the most obvious
things in the house.

Outside, the street is lit by its lamp posts, the crossing has a light over it,
and there is a porch light over the front step.

*[Press it again]*

Press it again for daylight. Everything else works exactly the same.

## 11. The balance exercise  (~55 s)

*[The balance task]*

The last exercise is balance and reach.

You stand still and look ahead. After a countdown, a glowing ball appears at
arm's length, and you reach out and touch it — only as far as is comfortable. If
you cannot reach it, that is fine; it simply waits.

There are three of them: one straight ahead, one to the left, one to the right.

While you reach, the headset's own movement tells us how far your head
travelled. That is the measure — steadiness, not speed. Reaching past your
balance point is one of the most common ways people go over, so the number that
matters is how little you moved, not how fast you got there.

It is a guide drawn from headset movement, not a medical measurement, and the
app says so.

## 12. How it is scored  (~140 s)

*[The debrief panel]*

Now the scoring, because it is the part that makes this training rather than a
walkthrough.

Every area is scored out of a hundred on its own.

For a room, it is fifty per cent the hazards you found in that room, thirty per
cent how cleanly you walked the route, and twenty per cent whether you used the
rail where there is one. Any part a room cannot test is dropped, and the rest is
rescaled — so a room with no rail is scored on hazards and route alone.

The street is sixty per cent hazards found outdoors and forty per cent the
pavement route.

The road crossing is its own test: sixty-five per cent how your crossings went,
and thirty-five per cent how you stepped off the kerb.

*[Show the crossing breakdown]*

A crossing is scored like this. Fifty points for getting across before the
traffic got its green back — forty of those if you finished on the flashing man,
which is normal and correct. Twenty-five for setting off promptly once the green
man appeared, because hesitating is what runs the clock out. And twenty-five for
staying between the white lines the whole way over.

Stepping off the kerb is scored separately: thirty-five for waiting for a steady
green man, twenty-five for looking both ways first, twenty for crossing at the
markings, and twenty for leaving enough of a gap in the traffic.

*[Show the balance line]*

Balance is the steadiness of the reaches you completed, multiplied by the
fraction you completed. A reach you ran out of time on does not drag the
steadiness down — standing still would otherwise score perfectly — it lowers the
completion fraction instead.

*[Show the near-miss line]*

There is one penalty. If you step onto a hazard you had not yet spotted, that is
a near miss, and it costs ten points from that room, capped at twenty. Stepping
on something you had already flagged does not count against you — you knew it
was there.

And flagging something safe is never penalised at all. It is recorded for the
researcher and that is all.

*[The overall score]*

The overall score is fifty per cent hazard recognition, thirty per cent rail
use, and twenty per cent balance.

## 13. Progress and the next session  (~55 s)

*[The performance board on the wall]*

At the end you get a plain-language debrief: what you missed, what to do about
it, and your score for every area.

All of it is saved. The board on the wall shows last session beside today's, so
improvement is visible rather than asserted.

And the system looks at your last three sessions, finds the area where your
average is lowest, and makes it the focus of your next one. A yellow light
points you to it when you start, and it reminds you what you missed there last
time.

Once every area is at ninety or above, there is no focus. You are told to
explore freely.

## 14. Close  (~25 s)

*[Wide shot of the house, then the title card]*

Twenty-seven hazards, seven safe objects, seven rooms, three walking exercises
and a balance test — all of it scored, all of it recorded, all of it comparable
between sessions.

Because this is not a test you pass once. It is practice you get better at.

Thank you for watching.

## Ninety-second cut-down

Use sections 1, 2, 9 and 12. From section 2, keep only the three paragraphs that
begin "First", "Second" and "Third". From section 12, keep only the first two
paragraphs and the overall score.

## Delivery notes

- Pause at the end of every section. Each pause is an edit point.
- Say the numbers slowly: *twenty-seven hazards, seven safe objects, sixty per
  cent*.
- Do not read on-screen text aloud while it is on screen. Let it be read.
- Flag each hazard on camera as you name it, so the spoken name and the floating
  label appear together.
- If a take goes wrong, redo that section rather than the whole run.
